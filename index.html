Soft Pneumatic Inspection Robot for Environmental Protection.

SPIRo Engineering Notebook

SPIRo Project Github

SPIRo, short for soft pipe inspection robot, was born from my personal experience with a gas leak explosion. Further investigating the dangers of faulty gas pipelines online, I discovered, in the US, half a million undetected leaks release 7.1 million tons of methane annually. However, existing leak detection methods remain costly, inefficient, and difficult to deploy at scale due to the harsh environments pipelines are found in. Together with my brother Evan, I developed SPIRo, using emerging soft robot technology, allowing it to adaptively navigate pipelines while using its sensors and an ML framework to scan for leaks.

SPIRo is fully 3D printed, and we custom-designed the robot from scratch in CAD, from its magnetic grippers to its flexible scissor linkage. To enable faster movement, SPIRo is equipped with McKibben artificial muscles that pneumatically inflate and deflate to push the robot’s scissor linkage. It follows an inch-worm inspired movement. SPIRo’s magnetic feet also alternate adhering and lifting away from the pipe, allowing the expanded scissor linkage to shift forward. A similar thing happens for vertical locomotion.

I developed a custom circuit using MOSFETs that regulates the opening and closing of the pneumatic valves that control the flow of air to and from each of SPIRo’s actuators, modeling its movement. The first iteration of SPIRo was equipped with two gas composition sensors for methane and carbon monoxide, where the data was processed by a simple artificial neural network to determine the presence of a leak. However, during outside at-home tests, I discovered that wind would blow the gas plume away from the sensors, leading to false negatives. Thus, I equipped SPIRo with a thermal camera as well, allowing it to “see” the rising gas plumes. These thermal images were processed with CNNs, while the gas sensors were processed with ANNs, before their predictions were finally fused.

After at home tests, I conducted rigorous real-field testing at the Methane Emissions Technology Evaluation Center collecting over 20,000 data points. One shortcoming that I noticed from these field tests was that the ML architecture I currently had implemented was too large and inefficient. Thus, using these learnings, I sought to develop a new, more efficient architecture through reducing model size. That led me to looking deeper into the CNNs that I was currently using. Since CNNs are pre-trained and developed on larger datasets such as ImageNet, I was curious to see if these full architectures were actually the best for detecting gas leaks from thermal images, or if only using a subsection of the layers was sufficient, which would save computation time. In fact, removing layers would help to prevent overfitting and improve accuracy too! I dissected each of the CNNs that I was using and analyzed the features extracted by each layer. I was fascinated by how different CNNs and their inner layers reacted to an infrared image of a gas leak, with some placing more emphasis on the color contrasts between the warmer (leak) regions and the cooler (no leak) regions, while others highlighted the boundary between the two regions. I decided to take a diverse set of the represented features from different CNNs to improve robustness, and this new architecture achieved an accuracy of 95% on field data. It is also one of the highlights of my journal paper!

SPIRo has gained the attention of entrepreneurs through winning the Conrad Challenge, and we are currently expanding our startup Enviro-AI to distribute SPIRo around the world, reaching out to venture capitalists from MIT, Equinor, and the 1517 Fund. I hope SPIRo will be on every pipeline around the world in the coming years.

I have co-founded a start-up, Enviro-AI, to bring this product to market.

AI Copilot for Trauma Surgery

Access to life-saving humanitarian care in natural and man-made emergency conditions is challenging due to limited equipment and medical expertise. AI copilots are transforming surgery by providing real-time assistance, enhancing precision, and improving patient outcomes. I proposed a memory-augmented vision-language model (VLM) for surgical action recognition and anticipation to assist medical decision-making.

The proposed architecture consists of two modules: the vision module and the language module. The vision module is based on the MViTv2 transformer, and its outputs are fed into the language module. Because medical actions are often consecutive, with one action consistently performed after another, the language module is augmented with memory of previous actions through concatenating all past actions recognized or anticipated by the vision module. The language module, based on the FlanT5 Large Language Model (LLM) architecture, makes the final prediction on the recognized or anticipated action.

The Trauma THOMPSON dataset is employed for model training and validation. The format of the annotation is two words: a verb followed by a noun - for example, with “take being the verb and swab being the “noun”. The train-test split is 80-20.

The proposed architecture achieved a Top-1 accuracy of 69.11% on action recognition and 62.13% on anticipation, outperforming the results reported by previous literature.

My work is featured in Purdue IE News.

Algal Blooms research at SSTP

Harmful algal blooms (HABs), driven by environmental pollution, pose significant threats to water quality, public health, and aquatic ecosystems. This study enhances the prediction of HABs in Lake Erie, part of the Great Lakes system, by utilizing ensemble machine learning (ML) models coupled with explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) for interpretability. Using water quality data from 2013 to 2020, various physical, chemical, and biological parameters were analyzed to predict chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) concentrations, which are a commonly used indicator of phytoplankton biomass and a proxy for algal blooms. This study employed multiple ensemble ML models, including random forest (RF), deep forest (DF), gradient boosting (GB), and XGBoost, and compared their performance against individual models, such as support vector machine (SVM), decision tree (DT), and multi-layer perceptron (MLP). The findings revealed that the ensemble models, particularly XGBoost and deep forest (DF), achieved superior predictive accuracy, with R2 values of 0.8517 and 0.8544, respectively. The application of SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAPs) provided insights into the relative importance of the input features, identifying the particulate organic nitrogen (PON), particulate organic carbon (POC), and total phosphorus (TP) as the critical factors influencing the Chl-a concentrations. This research demonstrates the effectiveness of ensemble ML models for achieving high predictive accuracy, while the integration of XAI enhances model interpretability. The results support the development of proactive water quality management strategies and highlight the potential of advanced ML techniques for environmental monitoring.

FRC Robots at Harker

I was the software lead on Harker’s FIRST Robotics Competition (FRC) team from 2022 to 2024. These robots are built around a specific set of rules and regulations, and they accomplish different robot game missions from shooting and scoring game pieces to climbing at the end of a match.

In the 2023 season, our robot featured an angled elevator that extended forwards to reach three different scoring positions as well as a game piece collecting station. To control this elevator, I implemented PID motion control using the motor encoders of the robot, tuning this feedback controller to achieve smooth, accurate, and consistent extension distances. The robot also featured a swerve drivetrain, which I programmed using PIDs, gyroscopes, and feedforward motion controllers. Finally, I also developed a pitch alignment algorithm that helped the robot balance on a moving platform at the end of each match.

In the 2024 season, I continued to develop upon the swerve drivetrain, as well as implemented an adjustable shooter to shoot rings in two different scoring positions. The robot featured a ground intake, and the shooter was controlled with Limelight cameras that helped to align the robot with the target and calculate the velocity and angle at which a game piece would need to be shot in order to score through neural network keypoint detection algorithms.

FTC Robots designed from 2020 to 2023

I led the design and software code of two FIRST Tech Challenge Robots from 2020 to 2023 (Freight Frenzy and Power Play). Both are built around specific sets of rules, and they feature different approaches to manipulating game pieces to complete certain tasks, such as placing them on levels of shelves or stacking them on poles.

I innovated in several key software areas that helped to improve the reliability and consistency of our robot’s scoring and improve robot drivability. Primarily, I implemented command groups that automated the execution of continuous tasks, such as raising an elevator and scoring a game piece. I also innovated with PID controllers for our robot’s drivetrain and elevators, allowing for more precise control and movement to different heights and motor positions. I augmented the drivetrain with odometry wheels, an improvement over standard motor encoders that didn’t slide along the playing field, leading to more accurate readings. I also implemented Roadrunner and autonomous path planning to improve our team’s strategy and scoring during the autonomous period of the robot game. Finally, I implemented a computer-vision based object detection algorithm that dictated our team’s autonomous strategy based on the positioning of a randomized object. I also assisted in the CAD design of the robot, ensuring that communication between subteams was clear to streamline development.

During competitions, I also helped to resolve both mechanical and software issues. When our team’s main scoring mechanism, its elevator broke mid-match, I organized the team, addressing one small issue at a time, from repairing the snapped belt to returning the elevator before our next match, fixing the problem with minimal impact on our robot performance. I have led the team to reaching the division finals of the NorCal state championships.

HEVI (Houseless Environmental Volunteers Initiative)

Empowering youth to lead humanitarian and environmental volunteer projects in local communities.

I currently serve as the co-president of the Houseless Environmental Volunteers Initiative (HEVI), which aims to provide meaningful job opportunities with stipends to unhoused individuals in San Jose while furthering environmental efforts and contributing to the urban forest.

As the Bay Area recovers from the COVID-19 pandemic, the unhoused community has received inadequate support. The homelessness rate in California is projected to increase by 68% in the next 4 years, primarily due to the large number of layoffs caused by the COVID-19 pandemic and small business closures. Many people have been forced to foreclose their homes, couchsurf, or live on the streets. According to a survey conducted by Mercury News, homelessness is currently recognized as the number one issue of the Bay Area. HEVI’s mission is to address this severe crisis.

Environmental efforts have also been impeded throughout the pandemic. Government programs, individual donations, and volunteers have all been focused on the public health crisis. Small local nonprofits have shut down due to a lack of funding, and those that are still open are struggling to keep up.

HEVI aims to address both these issues by engaging the homeless community in reliable forms of employment that simultaneously support environmental initiatives. Together, HEVI has helped to plant over 2,000 trees, completed several biodiversity projects, and aided over 60 unhoused individuals.

As co-president, I have led several initiatives to improve HEVI’s community. I led the team to host quarterly dinner events at local homeless shelters in order to build a stronger bond and community within HEVI. Sharing conversations over meals has allowed me and the team to better understand the unique needs and stories of each of the unhoused, building supportive relationships with others. Simultaneously, I’ve led donation drives to these shelters, donating everything from essentials like clothes to fun board games

Inspired by many of the unhoused individuals’ stories, I’ve continued to fundraise for HEVI through door-to-door initiatives, using donations to support more unhoused individuals across San Jose with stipend opportunities. So far, we’ve raised over $5000 for HEVI, continuing to make an impact on those around me.

Led hands-on STEM sessions at local Title 1 elementary schools using LEGO robotics and coding games.

Wanting to expand the reach of FLL robotics to underserved communities, I cold-emailed 30 Title I schools across the Bay Area to bring robotics into the classroom, eventually partnering with the Henry Ford Elementary School and the Redwood City School District to bring hands-on STEM learning to the classroom. Meeting with school administrators, summer camp coordinators, and the current technology teacher to learn more about students’ interests and planning how to better create a fun learning environment for students, I designed a two week summer camp curriculum for 3rd to 5th graders across three elementary schools in the district. We received over 60 signups for our summer program.

Throughout the two weeks of teaching, although we initially started with quick presentations and structured building and coding activities to introduce the basics of robotics, we realized that the best method of promoting engagement was through encouraging the testing of unique ideas rather than providing prescribed instructions. Thus, after each student had grasped the very basics, I pivoted to a new teaching strategy: learning by doing. I supported each student by letting them experiment with new robot attachments, mechanisms, and code. When something went wrong, I helped the team of students brainstorm what went wrong and potential ideas to resolve the issue. This helped to foster a more interactive classroom that supported individual creativity and collaborative problem-solving. Many of our students stayed up to hours after class ended, only leaving when their parents arrived to pick them up. I’m grateful to have given so many students an opportunity to explore a passion for a new subject and build their confidence in their ability to create and innovate.

We’ve continued our program and have raised $4.5k to date to provide kits and materials for our classes, creating an after school enrichment program for FIRST robotics for Henry Ford’s students.

Led hands-on sessions building remote-control airplanes.

Invited to teach a paid remote-control airplane workshop to middle and elementary school students at a local library, my brother and I taught everything from the basics of the aerodynamics of flight to building RC airplanes from scratch using foam. We spent several hours helping each student refine their glider design through making cuts in the foam to optimize the angle of the leading edge of the wing and hot gluing everything together to ensure everything stayed in place. We then traveled to a nearby open field to test-fly the planes we had spent hours building. Yet, on our first flight, the plane struggled to gain altitude, eventually crashing into the grass nose-first. Together with each of the students, we brainstormed additional solutions from adding servos to control the pitch and roll of the plane to shifting around the center of gravity to prevent sudden pitch-up movements. These fixes proved successful, as our planes began to fly controlled and smoothly through the air. After building these foam planes, we also showed each of the students how they could design their own gliders through CAD and 3D printing. Each student designed a model of their favorite plane in CAD, and we spent a lot of time experimenting with them in the parking lot through adding and removing horizontal and vertical stabilizers to observe their effect on flight.
